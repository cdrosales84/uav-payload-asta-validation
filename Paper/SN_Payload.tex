\abstract{This work addresses the computational modeling, design, and simulation of a robust adaptive cascaded controller for last-mile delivery using quadrotors (UAVs), which carry a payload. The outer loop compensates cable motion relative to the inclination required by the reference acceleration. The system under study consists of the quadrotor, the cable, and the payload, where these elements are modeled as a subactuated dynamic system in which the payload must follow a desired trajectory and the quadrotor acts as the actuation platform. Taking advantage of the geometric and structural equilibrium inherent in the pendulum-type configuration, the proposed controller combines a kinematic controller (outer loop), which shapes the UAV velocity command to dampen payload oscillations, with a dynamic compensator (inner loop), which tracks that command by an adaptive super-twisting law. The proposed controller ensures simplicity and robustness during control. Stability analysis was performed using Lyapunov theory, demonstrating the practical stability of control errors and ensuring the overall stability of the closed-loop system. The numerical simulations presented for a quadrotor carrying a suspended payload validate the proposed controller, demonstrating that the proposal is effective in rejecting disturbances and ensuring precise trajectory tracking, making it ideal for high-precision UAV applications.}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{State of the Art and Control of Cable-Suspended Payload Transport}
Cable-suspended payload transport via unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) is a cornerstone of modern aerial logistics, infrastructure inspection, and emergency response in complex environments. Despite its operational potential, high-precision transport using a single UAV remains an open challenge. The system is fundamentally underactuated: a suspended load introduces oscillatory degrees of freedom that cannot be directly controlled, as the number of independent control inputs does not increase with the payload’s dimensionality. In this coupled quadcopter–cable–payload system, payload oscillations and external factors, such as wind gusts, act as primary sources of dynamic unbalanced that break the system’s natural equilibrium. These external perturbations not only degrade tracking accuracy but also introduce directional biases in the system's response, exacerbated by the nonlinear coupling \cite{Estevez2024,Wang2023,Yu2024,Mohammadi2021}.

Two configurations are commonly considered. Cooperative transport with several quadcopters distributes the load over more actuators and can improve transport performance \cite{Costantini2024,sun2025agile,di2026modeling}, where these systems require advanced, nonlinear control techniques to reject disturbances \cite{han2022controller} and avoid obstacles \cite{aliyu2022control}, whereas single-UAV transport keeps the hardware, sensing, and coordination requirements minimal. Since the cooperative case and its drawbacks are discussed later in this section, the present work focuses on the single-vehicle configuration, in which one higher-performance vehicle must carry the suspended load along the desired trajectory. In much of the literature, the suspended load is treated as a disturbance or an undesirable effect to be rejected.

For single-vehicle transport, where quadcopters today play a significant role in logistics and rescue operations, most control designs are built upon a dynamic model of the coupled quadcopter--payload--cable system. A geometric controller on the Lie group SE(3) \cite{goodarzi2014geometric} avoids the singularities of Euler-angle parameterizations. Its performance, however, is tied to an accurate full-dynamics model and full-state feedback, so that wind gusts and parametric uncertainty, explicitly acknowledged by the authors, directly degrade tracking, while the scheme provides no online mechanism to estimate or compensate for unmodeled effects. To improve disturbance handling, robust controllers \cite{qian2019path,barikbin2019trajectory} preserve tracking accuracy under bounded disturbances. Nevertheless, robustness is typically obtained through conservative worst-case gains tuned around fixed operating points, which limits performance under time-varying disturbances and, as the authors themselves note, leaves part of the load swing uncompensated. Passivity-based strategies, such as an energy-shaping controller \cite{guerrero2015passivity}, exploit the system's energy structure to attenuate load oscillations. This approach, however, requires an accurate energy and parameter model and generally yields only passive asymptotic damping, whose convergence is slow against persistent excitation.

Sliding-mode control \cite{kusznir2020sliding} is well suited to rejecting matched uncertainties and reducing payload vibration. Yet conventional first-order implementations act at the UAV position or attitude level, induce chattering that stresses the actuators, and rely on prior knowledge of the disturbance bounds. Optimization- and planning-based methods, such as optimal trajectory tracking \cite{akhtar2022path} and swing-free trajectory generation \cite{palunko2012trajectory}, reduce oscillations by shaping the reference itself. Their largely feedforward nature, however, makes them sensitive to model mismatch and unanticipated disturbances, and the associated optimization introduces a computational burden that is difficult to sustain on lightweight embedded platforms.

Beyond the single-vehicle case, transporting loads that exceed the capacity of one drone has motivated cooperative systems \cite{maza2009multi,michael2011cooperative}, in which coordination and joint manipulation are essential. These gains, however, come at the cost of additional hardware, distributed sensing, and inter-agent coordination, which restrict their applicability in the low-cost, single-UAV missions targeted in this work.

A related line refines the physical realism of the model: cable-tension management to prevent the instantaneous loss of controllability under slack cables \cite{delbene2024cables}, geometric control accounting for cable elasticity \cite{goodman2023geometric}, and quantification of the tether effect on UAV stability through Lyapunov exponents \cite{tang2024analyzing}. Valuable as they are, these contributions increase the model fidelity and state-estimation requirements, and they remain primarily analysis- or modeling-oriented rather than delivering a lightweight feedback law for real-time transport.

A second group manages uncertainty through intelligent or adaptive compensation, including optimized neural PID controllers under unknown dynamics and external disturbances \cite{siwek2024application} and robust architectures tolerant to unknown payload mass \cite{outeiro2023control}. Learning-based schemes, however, depend on representative training data and seldom provide formal stability guarantees, whereas mass-robust designs typically address a single uncertain parameter rather than the combined effect of parametric drift, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.

Iterative learning control (ILC) is another adaptive approach for uncertain systems. It refines the input from one execution of a task to the next, and it has been applied to precise quadrotor tracking of repeated or periodic trajectories \cite{schAR12,han2025sliding}. Recent adaptive ILC designs extend the method to effects that a deployed vehicle also faces. One design \cite{shen2025neural} treats strict-feedback systems with unknown state delays and input saturation by combining radial-basis-function neural networks, Lyapunov--Krasovskii functionals, command filtering, and auxiliary saturation-compensation systems. A subsequent design \cite{shen2026equilibrium} introduces update laws with an equilibrium factor that tolerate initial-state errors and actuator faults in robot arms. In both designs, convergence is established along the iteration axis through a composite energy function, which presupposes a task repeated over a finite interval. A delivery flight is executed once, along a reference that changes with each mission. The dynamic compensator of Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} therefore adapts its parameters along the time axis, within a single flight. Input saturation, delay, and actuator faults, which these ILC designs treat explicitly, are not included in the present analysis (Section~\ref{sec:conclusiones}).

A third group operates at the planning and coordination level: two-time-scale swing minimization \cite{costantini2025two}, distributed trajectory optimization for scalability \cite{jackson2020scalable}, safe formation transport via ``virtual tubes'' \cite{liu2026virtual}, and SQP-based tension allocation for energy efficiency and slack avoidance \cite{vazquez:hal-05485942}. These methods presuppose either a clear time-scale separation, a multi-UAV formation, or the online solution of constrained optimization problems, all of which raise the computational and structural demands beyond what a single embedded platform can typically afford. Finally, perception- and orientation-aware controllers that decouple translational motion from yaw to preserve situational awareness during transport \cite{masuda2026geometric} target viewpoint regulation rather than payload-swing reduction, and thus pursue an objective that is complementary to, but distinct from, the one addressed here.

Recent work on cable-suspended transport can be broadly grouped into three main directions. The first includes fully coupled nonlinear dynamic controllers, such as geometric, passivity-based, or model-based schemes derived from complete Newton--Euler or Lagrangian descriptions of the UAV--payload system \cite{Estevez2024,Costantini2024}, which reach high accuracy when the model is reliable but require detailed knowledge of payload mass, cable geometry, and tension conditions, together with rich state information and nontrivial computational effort. The second comprises robust and adaptive sliding-mode strategies, including higher-order and super-twisting algorithms \cite{Wang2023,Mohammadi2021,rao2024adaptive}, which improve disturbance rejection but are usually applied at the position or attitude level, with limited integration into a payload-aware reference layer and only partial validation on real suspended-load transport. The third includes optimization-based and predictive methods, such as swing-aware trajectory planning and nonlinear MPC \cite{Yu2024,Estevez2024}, which handle constraints and reduce swing at the planning stage but rely on accurate models and computational resources that may hinder deployment on lightweight embedded platforms.

A hybrid regulator \cite{budak2026hybrid} combines a linear quadratic regulator with sliding-mode and super-twisting action, tuned by the Big Bang–Big Crunch algorithm, to improve transient performance and limit chattering in a three-degree-of-freedom levitation system that does not carry a suspended load.

Recent advancements in agile aerial manipulation, such as the cinodynamic planning proposed in \cite{sun2025agile}, have pushed the boundaries of maneuverability. However, there is still a gap in providing lightweight, adaptive solutions that handle the external perturbations of wind and uncertain payload masses without the high computational cost of nonlinear model predictive control.

\subsection{Research gap and study contributions}

Although the reviewed studies have achieved notable advances in the control of UAV cable-suspended payload transport systems, Table~\ref{tab:method_comparison} shows that they do not combine, in one single-UAV law, a payload-level objective, feedback swing compensation, and an adaptive velocity loop without an online optimization. The controller of Section~\ref{sec:kinematic} is proposed for that combination of design choices. The kinematic controller places the objective on the payload and compensates swing through the velocity command. The dynamic compensator tracks that command with an adaptive super-twisting law. The evaluation in this paper is the deterministic simulation study of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}. That study does not establish robustness under general uncertainty, adaptation to payload mass, or suitability for limited onboard sensing and computation.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

\begin{itemize}

 \item \textbf{Payload-oriented kinematic control.} We place the tracking objective on the payload and derive a swing-compensation term from the kinematic relation between the cable attitude and the UAV velocity. The term enters the velocity command. On the ascending circular reference, the maneuver is completed with this term and is not completed without it.

 \item \textbf{Swing compensation about the quasi-static equilibrium.} The outer loop separates the cable inclination required by the reference acceleration from the oscillation about that inclination, and it compensates only the latter. In the reported runs, the swing signals remain bounded after the initial transient. These runs do not show that the tracking error stays uniform under arbitrary disturbances.

 \item \textbf{Adaptive super-twisting velocity loop.} The inner loop tracks the kinematic velocity command in the presence of parametric mismatch and external disturbances. Its adaptation law updates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v\). Theorem~\ref{th:inner} gives uniform ultimate boundedness of the sliding variable, the super-twisting state, and the parameter errors when the disturbance derivative is bounded. The simulations do not sweep payload mass, so adaptation to payload mass is not shown.

 \item \textbf{Architectural comparison.} Swing compensation remains in the kinematic command, and the treatment of parametric mismatch remains in the adaptive velocity loop. Relative to a fully coupled nonlinear design, the feedback law does not embed the full UAV--cable--payload model. Relative to nonlinear model predictive control, the command is analytical and involves no online optimization. That comparison concerns the structure of the law. It is not a measured result on embedded hardware, sensing, or computation time. Relative to a first-order sliding-mode velocity loop, Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_smc} reports one deterministic comparison on the circular trajectory.

 \item \textbf{Separate stability arguments and deterministic simulation.} Theorem~\ref{th:outer} treats the outer loop under exact velocity tracking, and Theorem~\ref{th:inner} treats the inner loop. A proof that the interconnection preserves those margins is left open. The numerical evidence consists of deterministic scenarios: the circular ablation, one lemniscate trajectory with two prescribed wind intervals acting on the UAV, the cable, and the payload, and the inner-loop benchmark. In the wind scenario, payload tracking and swing remain bounded during the two prescribed intervals. Sensor noise, delay, actuator saturation, computational cost, and physical experiments are outside the present evidence.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the proposed methodology, including the geometric modeling and the proposed controller. Section~\ref{sec:simulations} reports the simulation results. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:conclusiones} concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}


\subsection{System Model}\label{sec:geom_model}

The geometric relationship between the UAV and the suspended payload is first introduced to formulate the kinematic controller (outer loop) at the payload level. Figure \ref{fig:drone_carga} shows the payload transport system, with the defined variables and reference frames.
% REV4-C3: Unificar "Figure" con el resto de citas. Si se adopta "Fig.", cambiar aquí a "Fig.~\ref{fig:drone_carga}".
Let $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ denote the UAV position and $\mathbf{x}_l\in\mathbb{R}^3$ the payload position, both expressed in the inertial frame. The cable vector is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_l,
\label{eq:cable_vector}
\end{equation}
where $l=\|\mathbf{h}\|$ is the cable length, assumed constant.

\begin{figure}

    \caption{Geometry of the quadrotor with a cable-suspended payload. The inertial positions of the vehicle and of the payload are $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}_l$, and the cable vector is $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_l$.}
    \label{fig:drone_carga}
\end{figure}

For geometric interpretation, the relative payload position may also be described in a body-related frame attached to the UAV. Let ${}^{b}\mathbf{r}_l\in\mathbb{R}^3$ denote the vector from the UAV to the payload expressed in that frame. Then, the payload position in the inertial frame can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_l = \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{R}_{\psi}\,{}^{b}\mathbf{r}_l,
\label{eq:payload_position_yaw}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}_{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is the rotation matrix associated with the UAV yaw angle $\psi$, namely,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_{\psi}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\
\sin\psi & \cos\psi  & 0\\
0        & 0         & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:yaw_rotation}
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:payload_position_yaw} yields
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_l
=
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
+
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{\psi}\,{}^{b}\mathbf{r}_l
+
\mathbf{R}_{\psi}\,{}^{b}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_l.
\label{eq:payload_velocity_yaw}
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:payload_position_yaw}--\eqref{eq:payload_velocity_yaw} show that the payload motion is geometrically influenced by the vehicle heading. This representation is useful to interpret the relative UAV--payload configuration, particularly in the horizontal plane. Nevertheless, the proposed swing-compensation mechanism does not explicitly use the yaw variable. The reason is that oscillation attenuation is primarily governed by horizontal translational motion and cable-state variation, whereas the yaw angle does not provide direct information relevant to the swing-reduction objective considered here.

Therefore, for control design and implementation purposes, the compact inertial-frame representation obtained directly from \eqref{eq:cable_vector} is adopted, namely,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_l = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{h},
\qquad
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_l = \dot{\mathbf{x}} - \dot{\mathbf{h}}.
\label{eq:payload_velocity_compact}
\end{equation}
This form retains the essential geometric information required for payload-level trajectory tracking and swing attenuation while avoiding unnecessary coordinate transformations.


As a first step, the dynamic model of the vehicle is presented. The one proposed in \cite{Lucas_ICUAS2014}, formulated for a commercial drone with internal stabilization loops, was adopted, since it directly provides the mapping between the normalized input commands and the vehicle accelerations, a relationship that constitutes the basis for the design of the dynamic compensator.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ModeloLucas}
    \dot{\mathbf{v}} = \boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\mathbf{u} - \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\mathbf{v} +\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)
\end{equation}

The velocity commands are applied in the vehicle's yaw frame, the frame in which the model of \cite{Lucas_ICUAS2014} is defined. Let $\mathbf{R}_\psi^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ be the upper-left block of \eqref{eq:yaw_rotation}. The horizontal velocity and the horizontal command of the kinematic controller, derived in Section~\ref{sec:kinematic}, are expressed in that frame,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yaw_frame}
\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{R}_\psi^{h})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{x}}_h,\qquad
\mathbf{v}_d = (\mathbf{R}_\psi^{h})^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{c,h},\qquad \mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_d\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
\end{equation}
The dynamic compensator acts on these $n=2$ channels. The vertical and yaw channels receive $v_{c,z}$ and $\dot\psi_c$ directly. For each channel $i\in\{x,y\}$, \eqref{eq:ModeloLucas} reads $\dot v_i=\kappa_{u,i}u_i-\kappa_{v,i}v_i+\delta_i$, with $\kappa_{u,i},\kappa_{v,i}>0$. The disturbance $\delta_i$ captures the cable force, aerodynamic forces, frame rotation, and unmodelled dynamics. Each channel is linear in the parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i=[\,1/\kappa_{u,i}\ \ \kappa_{v,i}/\kappa_{u,i}\,]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$.
By hypothesis, it is assumed that the derivatives of this perturbation are bounded. Therefore, $\|\dot{ \boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\| \leq \delta_o$,being a positive constant.


\subsection{Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}

Assumptions~\ref{as:physical} and~\ref{as:parameters} are used in the dynamic compensator. Assumptions~\ref{as:estimation}--\ref{as:vertical} are used in the kinematic controller.

\begin{assumption}\label{as:physical}
The disturbance term $\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)$ acting on the velocity dynamics of the UAV and its time derivative are bounded, i.e., $\|\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\|\le\delta_o$ and $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\|\le\bar{\delta}$, and the normalized actuator commands operate within their linear (unsaturated) range during nominal operation. Physically, this assumption is justified by the finite energy of atmospheric disturbances. Wind gusts in urban delivery environments exhibit bounded magnitudes and limited rates of change, ensuring that the control gains and adaptation laws remain within feasible operational ranges.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Fast cable-rate estimation]\label{as:estimation}
The two-stage estimator \eqref{eq:h_raw}--\eqref{eq:h_p_filter} operates on a fast time scale ($\varepsilon_1,\tau_h$ small), so that the estimation residual
$\boldsymbol{\xi}_e=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_p-\dot{\mathbf{h}}$ is bounded and decays to a small neighborhood of the origin after a fast transient. This assumption is justified by the separation of time-scales between the high-frequency estimation loop and the slower mechanical response of the UAV. By selecting sufficiently small filter time constants ($(\epsilon_1, \tau_h)$), the estimation residual becomes negligible relative to the control sampling period, enabling a decoupled analysis of the task-space tracking error
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Quasi-static small inclination]\label{as:smallangle}
The horizontal cable displacement satisfies $\|\mathbf{h}_h\|/l\ll 1$, so that the
equilibrium reference $\mathbf{h}_h^\star=(l/g)\,\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{d,h}$ is exact to first order and the higher-order pendular terms are negligible. This assumption is consistent with the operational requirements of last-mile delivery, where cargo integrity is prioritized. By avoiding aggressive maneuvers, the system remains within a near-equilibrium regime that preserves the validity of the first-order pendular model while ensuring the safety of sensitive payloads during transport.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Smooth bounded reference]\label{as:reference}
The reference trajectory is feasible and sufficiently smooth, with
$\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_d,\dddot{\mathbf{x}}_d\in\mathcal{L}_\infty$. Consequently
$\mathbf{h}_h^\star$ and $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star$ are well defined and bounded. Physically, this bounded smoothness requirement ensures that the reference motion does not demand infinite motor torque or instantaneous thrust changes. It reflects real-world flight planning where jerk and acceleration are limited to satisfy actuator constraints and avoid exciting the higher-order structural modes of the flexible cable.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Vertical decoupling]\label{as:vertical}
The vertical cable motion $\dot{h}_z$ is regulated by the inner loop and remains
bounded; hence, the swing-compensation analysis is restricted to the horizontal
subspace $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\in\mathbb{R}^2$. Vertical decoupling is justified by the quadrotor’s high thrust-to-weight ratio along the vertical axis ($(z_b)$). Since the vehicle possesses direct actuation to compensate for gravity and vertical payload reactions, the translational swing dynamics are primarily governed by horizontal accelerations, justifying a focused analysis on the horizontal swing-reduction subspace.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}\label{as:parameters}
The ideal Parameter matrices of the model, denoted as $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v$, are bounded such that $ \Vert{}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\Vert{} \leq \kappa_{uM} $ and $\Vert{}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\Vert{} \leq \kappa_{vM}$, where $ \kappa_{uM} $ and $ \kappa_{vM} $ are a known positive constant.
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Kinematic Controller}\label{sec:kinematic}

This paper presents a hierarchical, payload-oriented control system for a drone. The study aims to track the desired trajectory and minimize oscillations of the suspended payload during the task. The proposed controller is a robust adaptive cascaded scheme with two loops (Figure~\ref{fig:diagrama_controlador}). The kinematic controller (outer loop) makes the payload follow the prescribed trajectory and reduces its swing; its output is the velocity command \(\mathbf{u}_k\) derived below. The dynamic compensator (inner loop) does not suppress swing directly. It tracks \(\mathbf{u}_k\) despite model uncertainty and external disturbances, and its control law is an adaptive super-twisting law. The loops interact through this command: the outer-loop analysis assumes \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}_k\), and the inner loop enforces that condition approximately. If the command is not realized, the kinematic compensation is not executed and the resulting velocity error can excite the payload. These names are used throughout the paper.

This section describes the kinematic controller that enables the payload to follow a trajectory and minimizes its oscillations. The operating assumptions are stated in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, before the control laws derived below. The higher-level modules (mission management and trajectory planning) are not part of the controller synthesis; they only provide a feasible payload reference along with the associated operational constraints.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{Figures/diagrama_controlador.eps}
    \caption{Block diagram of the proposed controller. The kinematic controller builds the velocity command $\mathbf{u}_k$ from the payload reference, the payload state, and the cable motion. The dynamic compensator tracks $\mathbf{u}_k$.}
    \label{fig:diagrama_controlador}
\end{figure}

It is important to note that the UAV's yaw angle is not specifically considered in the proposed payload control strategy. This is because the payload's dynamic behavior is primarily affected by the UAV's translational motion, while yaw has a negligible effect on the suspended payload's roll motion. Therefore, yaw is controlled separately using a direct reference tracking controller, which is decoupled from the kinematic controller.
The kinematic controller is formulated at the payload level so that the suspended load, rather than the UAV itself, tracks the prescribed reference trajectory. Let $\mathbf{x}_d\in\mathbb{R}^3$ denote the desired payload position, and let $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d$ and $\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_d$ denote the corresponding reference velocity and acceleration. The payload tracking error is defined as
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}_l,
\label{eq:payload_tracking_error}
\end{equation}
with time derivative
\begin{equation}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}} = \dot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{x}}_l.
\label{eq:payload_tracking_error_dot}
\end{equation}
A nominal translational command is first introduced as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{base}}
=
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d
+
\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star\\ 0\end{bmatrix}
+
K_{\mathrm{traj}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},
\label{eq:base_reference}
\end{equation}
where $K_{\mathrm{traj}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is a positive definite diagonal gain matrix and $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star$ is the quasi-static cable rate of \eqref{eq:href}. Since $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_l+\mathbf{h}$, the UAV velocity that keeps the payload on its reference with the cable at the quasi-static inclination is $\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^\star$; the added term supplies the horizontal part of that velocity. Although \eqref{eq:base_reference} ensures payload-level tracking, it does not compensate for the oscillations of the pendular configuration. Such compensation requires the cable vector $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_l$ together with its rate $\dot{\mathbf{h}}$. Only $\mathbf{h}$ is measured; $\dot{\mathbf{h}}$ is reconstructed by a two-stage cable-rate estimator. A high-gain stage first produces a raw velocity estimate from the tracking error $\mathbf{h}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}$, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h_raw} \mathbf{h}_{p,\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_1}\,K_1\big(\mathbf{h}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}\big), 
\end{equation} 
with $K_1\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ positive definite and $\varepsilon_1>0$. The internal state $\hat{\mathbf{h}}$ is then driven by the \emph{filtered} rate estimate $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_p$, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h_hat_dyn} \dot{\hat{\mathbf{h}}} = \hat{\mathbf{h}}_p, \end{equation} 
while $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_p$ is obtained by low-pass filtering the raw signal, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h_p_filter} \tau_h\,\dot{\hat{\mathbf{h}}}_p + \hat{\mathbf{h}}_p = \mathbf{h}_{p,\mathrm{raw}}, \qquad \tau_h>0. 
\end{equation} 
In this closed-loop structure, a smaller $\varepsilon_1$ yields faster tracking of the measurement (and thus a more responsive raw velocity estimate), whereas the time constant $\tau_h$ attenuates the noise amplified by differentiation. The filtered output $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_p$ is the estimate of $\dot{\mathbf{h}}$ used in the feedback law; at steady state $\hat{\mathbf{h}}\to\mathbf{h}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_p\to\dot{\mathbf{h}}$. The selection of $\varepsilon_1$ and $\tau_h$ therefore reflects a trade-off between estimation velocity and noise rejection. Since the dominant oscillations are excited by horizontal accelerations, only the horizontal cable component is compensated. Let $\mathbf{h}_h=[h_x,h_y]^T$ denote the horizontal cable displacement and $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}=[\hat{h}_{p,x}, \hat{h}_{p,y}]^T$ its estimated rate. Under the convention $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_l$, the quasi-static cable reference and its rate are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:href}
\mathbf{h}_h^\star = \frac{l}{g}\,\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{d,h},\qquad
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star = \frac{l}{g}\,\dddot{\mathbf{x}}_{d,h}.
\end{equation}
This expression represents the \emph{equilibrium} inclination that the pendulum must adopt for the payload to follow the prescribed trajectory; it is not an oscillation to be suppressed. Both quantities are evaluated analytically from the reference, which is three times differentiable by Assumption~\ref{as:reference}. With $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_l$, the horizontal force balance on the payload gives, to first order, $\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{l,h}=(g/l)\,\mathbf{h}_h$, so the UAV leads the payload in the direction of the reference acceleration. Here $g$ is the gravitational acceleration. The swing-compensation term is then defined as \begin{equation}\label{eq:swing_compensation} \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp}} = \begin{bmatrix} -K_h\big(\mathbf{h}_h-\mathbf{h}_h^\star\big) -K_{dh}\big(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}-\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star\big)\\[1mm] 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation} with $K_h,K_{dh}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}$ positive definite and diagonal. The first term is a restoring action toward $\mathbf{h}_h^\star$: by referencing the equilibrium inclination rather than the pure vertical ($\mathbf{h}_h=\mathbf{0}$), it penalizes only the genuine swing (i.e., the deviation between the actual cable inclination and the inclination justified by the maneuver) instead of the inclination that the commanded acceleration itself requires. The second term damps the pendulum transients. A smooth start-up factor $\sigma(t)=1-e^{-t/T_s}$, with $T_s>0$, prevents impulsive commands at the onset of the maneuver, so that \begin{equation}\label{eq:final_reference} \mathbf{v}_c = \sigma(t)\,\big(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{base}} + \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp}}\big). \end{equation} 
The yaw channel is treated separately, as it is not coupled to the dominant horizontal oscillations: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:yaw_reference} \dot{\psi}_c = \dot{\psi}_d + k_{\psi}\tanh\!\big(k_{\psi}^t\,e_{\psi}\big), \qquad e_{\psi}=\psi_d-\psi, \end{equation} 
with $k_{\psi},k_{\psi}^t>0$. The complete command of the kinematic controller is $\mathbf{u}_k=[\mathbf{v}_c^T,\ \dot{\psi}_c]^T$, which serves as the reference for the dynamic compensator presented in Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}.

To explicitly model the estimation effect in the control law, let $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h = \mathbf{h}_h - \mathbf{h}_h^*$ denote the horizontal cable inclination error, and let $\mathbf{e}_h = \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h} - \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h$ represent the bounded residual error of the two-stage rate estimator. Recognizing that $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h = \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h + \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^*$, the estimated cable velocity used in \eqref{eq:swing_compensation}  can be decomposed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_residuo} 
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h} = \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h + \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mathbf{e}_h
\end{equation}

\subsection{Outer-Loop Stability}
\label{sec:closed_loop_analysis}

The outer-loop error analysis is carried out under the perfect velocity-tracking
condition $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}_k$, so that the kinematic command is realized exactly by the inner loop. The analysis is supported by Assumptions~\ref{as:estimation}--\ref{as:vertical}, which formalize the operating regime already invoked informally in the controller design.

Closed-Loop Error Analysis: it is assumed that the inner (dynamic) loop is sufficiently fast such that, on the time scale of the kinematic loop, the cable rate responds approximately instantaneously to the velocity command, that is,

\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h
&\simeq \mathbf{v}_{comp,h} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_h,
\label{eq:xx1}
\end{align}

where $\boldsymbol\mu_h$ encapsulates what this order reduction discards: the inertial term $\ddot{\theta}$
of the pendulum (the fast dynamics not modeled by the reduction), wind, and errors of the inner loop with respect to $\mathbf{v}_c$. Substituting the definition of $\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp},h}$ \eqref{eq:swing_compensation} in $\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h$, leads to:

\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h
&=-K_h\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h-K_{dh}(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{h,p}-\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h) + \boldsymbol{\mu}_h,
\label{eq:xx2}
\end{align}

Substituting the observer relation $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h} = \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h + \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mathbf{e}_h$ (eq. \eqref{eq:error_residuo}) into the kinematic control equation  \eqref{eq:xx2}:

\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h = -K_h \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h - K_{dh} (\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h + \mathbf{e}_h) + \mu_h
\end{align}

Subtracting $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^*$ from both sides to express the dynamics in terms of the error $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h$:

\begin{align}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h = -K_h \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h - K_{dh} \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h - K_{dh} \mathbf{e}_h - \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mu_h
\end{align}

Grouping the terms containing $\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h$ on the left-hand side:

\begin{align}
(I + K_{dh}) \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h = -K_h \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h - K_{dh} \mathbf{e}_h - \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mu_h
\end{align}
 
Since $K_{dh}$ is a positive-definite diagonal matrix, the matrix $(I + K_{dh})$ is always invertible. Multiplying by $(I + K_{dh})^{-1}$:

\begin{align}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h = -(I + K_{dh})^{-1} K_h \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h + (I + K_{dh})^{-1} (-K_{dh} \mathbf{e}_h - \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mu_h)
\end{align}

We now define the effective gain $\bar{K}_h$ and the exogenous perturbation $\xi_h$ in a non-circular manner:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ganancia.perturbacion}
\left\{
\begin{aligned} 
\bar{K}_h &= (I + K_{dh})^{-1} K_h,\\
\xi_h &= (I + K_{dh})^{-1} (-K_{dh} \mathbf{e}_h - \dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^* + \mu_h)
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

We obtain the equivalent decoupled system:

\begin{align}\label{eq:nuevo_Kh}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h = -\bar{K}_h \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h + \xi_h
\end{align}

%\textcolor{red}{REVISE $K_{dh}$ (28)} 
where $\xi_h$ depends solely on bounded external perturbations ($\mathbf{e}_h$, $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^*$, $\mu_h$) and system parameters, completely eliminating the dependence on $\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h$.
Now, building on the framework already established in this Section,

\begin{equation}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{l}
=-K_{\mathrm{traj}}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_l
+
(\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp,h}}),
\label{eq:auxx2}
\end{equation}

Defining the observer error $\mathbf{e}_{h}$ (bounded, by the the filter eq.\eqref{eq:error_residuo}) as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{h}=\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}-\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h\quad\rightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}=\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h+\mathbf{e}_{h} 
\label{eq:filter_error}
\end{equation}

Taking into account \eqref{eq:filter_error} and doing the difference $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\nonumber\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp,h}}$ as:

\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\nonumber\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp,h}}&=&\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\Big(-K_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h -K_{dh}(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}-\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h)\Big)=...\\
\nonumber&=&\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h+K_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +K_{dh}(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{p,h}-\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h)=...\\
\nonumber&=&K_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +K_{dh}\mathbf{e}_{h}+K_{dh}\dot{\mathbf{h}}_{h}-K_{dh}\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h+\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h=...\\
&=&K_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h(K_{dh}+I)-K_{dh}\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h+K_{dh}\mathbf{e}_{h}
\label{eq:auxx2b}
\end{eqnarray}

Now, considering $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h=\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h$ and arranging the difference (second and third terms in the previous equation) expressed by:

\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h(K_{dh}+I)-K_{dh}\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h=\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h(K_{dh}+I)+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h
\label{eq:auxx3}
\end{eqnarray}
considering the term $\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h(K_{dh}+1)$ and eq. \eqref{eq:nuevo_Kh} and replacing in eq.\eqref{eq:auxx3}, we obtain:

\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h(K_{dh}+I)=(K_{dh}+I)(-\bar{K}_h\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h + \boldsymbol{\xi}_h)=-(K_{dh}+I)\bar{K}_h\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +(K_{dh}+I)\boldsymbol{\xi}_h
\label{eq:auxx4}
\end{eqnarray}

Using \eqref{eq:auxx4} and \eqref{eq:auxx3} and grouping terms in eq.\eqref{eq:auxx2b}, we obtain:

\begin{eqnarray}
\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\nonumber\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{comp,h}}&=&K_{h}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +\Big(-(K_{dh}+I)\bar{K}_h\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h +(K_{dh}+I)\boldsymbol{\xi}_h+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h\Big)+K_{dh}\mathbf{e}_{h}=...\\
&=&\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\Big[K_{h}-(\bar{K}_{h}(K_{dh}+I))\Big]+(K_{dh}+I)\boldsymbol{\xi}_h+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h+K_{dh}\mathbf{e}_{h}
\label{eq:auxx5}
\end{eqnarray}

Replacing \eqref{eq:auxx5} in \eqref{eq:auxx2}:

\begin{equation}
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{l}
=-K_{\mathrm{traj}}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_l
-K_{h}K_{dh}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h+\boldsymbol{\zeta},
\label{eq:cinematica2X}
\end{equation}
where $B=\begin{bmatrix} -K_h K_{dh} \\ 0_{1 \times 2} \end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2}$ and  $\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(K_{dh}+I)\boldsymbol{\xi}_h+\dot{\mathbf{h}}^*_h+K_{dh}\mathbf{e}_{h}$

Under perfect velocity tracking, substituting \eqref{eq:final_reference} into
\eqref{eq:payload_velocity_compact} and neglecting the start-up factor after the
initial transient ($\sigma(t)\to 1$) yields, from \eqref{eq:nuevo_Kh} and \eqref{eq:cinematica2X}, the \emph{first-order} cascade:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cl_payload}
\left\{
\begin{aligned} 
    \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_h
&= -\bar{K}_h\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h + \boldsymbol{\xi}_h,\\
\dot{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_l
&= -K_{\mathrm{traj}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_l
   + B\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h
   + \boldsymbol{\zeta},
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$ incorporates the horizontal action in the payload channel, $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h$ collects the bounded estimation residue of the assumption~\ref{as:estimation} together with the reference rate mismatch $\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h-\dot{\mathbf{h}}_h^\star$, and $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$ includes the bounded vertical contribution of the assumption~\ref{as:vertical} and the start-up residual term. The damping gain $K_{dh}$ does not appear in \eqref{eq:cl_payload} because it acts as a transient shaping practical term and is not required for stability, which is set later with $K_{dh}=0$.

The structure \eqref{eq:cl_payload} is a cascade of two
input-to-state stable (ISS) first-order subsystems, and is summarized in the following
result.

\begin{theorem}[Outer-loop stability]\label{th:outer}
Consider the closed-loop error dynamics \eqref{eq:cl_payload}
under Assumptions~\ref{as:estimation}--\ref{as:vertical}, with $K_h,K_{\mathrm{traj}}$
symmetric positive definite. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textnormal{(a)}] If $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h=\mathbf{0}$ and $\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\mathbf{0}$,
  the origin $(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h)=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})$ is
  local exponentially stable provided the gains satisfy
  \begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\min}(K_{\mathrm{traj}})>\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad
  \mu\,\lambda_{\min}(K_h)>\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|^2,
  \label{eq:gain_condition}
  \end{equation}
  for some weight $\mu>0$.
  \item[\textnormal{(b)}] If $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h$ and $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$ are bounded,
  the cascade is ISS: all error signals remain bounded and converge to a compact
  neighborhood of the origin whose size is proportional to
  $\sup_t\|(\boldsymbol{\xi}_h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Consider the weighted Lyapunov function
\begin{equation}
V=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
 +\tfrac{\mu}{2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h^\top\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h,
 \qquad \mu>0,
\label{eq:cl_lyapunov}
\end{equation}
which is positive definite and radially unbounded. Its time derivative along
\eqref{eq:cl_payload} is
\begin{equation}
\dot V=
-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top K_{\mathrm{traj}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
-\mu\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h^\top \bar{K}_h\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h
+\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top B\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h
+\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top\boldsymbol{\zeta}
+\mu\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h^\top\boldsymbol{\xi}_h.
\label{eq:cl_Vdot}
\end{equation}

\emph{Part (a).} With $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h=\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\mathbf{0}$, the cross term
is bounded by Young's inequality,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top B\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h
\le \tfrac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|^2
   +\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|^2\|\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\|^2,
\label{eq:young}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\dot V \le
-\Big(\lambda_{\min}(K_{\mathrm{traj}})-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big)\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|^2
-\Big(\mu\,\lambda_{\min}(K_h)-\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|^2\Big)\|\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\|^2.
\label{eq:cl_Vdot_bound}
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{eq:gain_condition} both coefficients are strictly positive, hence
$\dot V\le -\alpha V$ for some $\alpha>0$, 
 $\forall \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h \in \mathcal{D}$, where $\mathcal{D} = \{ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \Vert{}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\Vert{} \le h_{\max} \}$, being $h_{\max}$ the bound where the small-angle approximation is no longer valid. 

which proves local exponential stability of
the origin.

\emph{Part (b).} With bounded $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h,\boldsymbol{\zeta}$, applying Young's
inequality to the disturbance terms as well gives
\begin{equation}
\dot V \le
-c_1\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|^2
-c_2\|\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h\|^2
+\rho\big(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_h\|^2+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|^2\big),
\label{eq:cl_Vdot_iss}
\end{equation}
with $c_1,c_2,\rho>0$ obtained from \eqref{eq:gain_condition} with a margin reserved
for the disturbance bounds. Thus $\dot V<0$ outside a compact set, which establishes
input-to-state stability of the cascade with respect to
$(\boldsymbol{\xi}_h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})$. Equivalently, since subsystem
\eqref{eq:nuevo_Kh} is ISS with respect to $\boldsymbol{\xi}_h$ and subsystem
\eqref{eq:cl_payload} is ISS with respect to $(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})$, the cascade interconnection of ISS systems is ISS, recovering the same conclusion.
This proves part~(b) and completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Theorem~\ref{th:outer} makes explicit the design trade-off hidden in the original
formulation: the trajectory gain $K_{\mathrm{traj}}$ sets the payload-tracking rate,
while the swing gain $K_h$ must be large enough, relative to the coupling $\|B\|$ and
the chosen weight $\mu$, to dominate the restoring action injected into the payload
channel. The damping gain $K_{dh}$, although not required for stability, is retained in
the implementation to shape the pendular transient and reduce overshoot, as confirmed
by the simulation results.

\subsection{Dynamic Compensator}\label{sec:dynamic}

This section presents the dynamic compensator, which enforces the perfect velocity-tracking condition $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}_k$ assumed in the previous section, driving the velocity-tracking error to a small neighborhood of the origin despite model uncertainty and external disturbances. Its control law is an adaptive super-twisting law. Super-twisting is adopted because, as a second-order sliding-mode technique, it preserves the robustness and finite-time convergence of sliding-mode control while substantially attenuating the chattering associated with first-order sliding-mode controllers. In addition, an adaptation law for the model parameters is introduced to reduce the conservativeness of the control gains and improve tracking performance under varying payload conditions.


The kinematic controller commands are reference velocities $\mathbf{v}_d$. Therefore, the following sliding surface is proposed:
% REV3-C13/C14: Describir explícitamente las perturbaciones externas variantes en el tiempo usadas en simulación y distinguir casos periódicos y no periódicos. Esta hipótesis debe conectarse con los perfiles simulados.

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s} = \tilde{\mathbf{v}} + \boldsymbol{\beta}\int \tilde{\mathbf{v}} dt
\end{equation}
Deriving the above equation,

\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{s}} = \dot{\tilde{\mathbf{v}}} + \boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} = (\dot{\mathbf{v}} - \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d) + \boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_d)
\end{equation}

The equivalent control law in the sliding mode can be found by solving $\dot{\mathbf{s}}=0$. Therefore, \eqref{eq:ModeloLucas} is replaced in the above equation,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Condicion_S}
    \dot{\mathbf{s}} = \Big(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\mathbf{u} - \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\Big) - 
    \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} = 0
\end{equation}

clearing the control action $\mathbf{u}$, it can be expressed as:

\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:AccionIdeal}
    \mathbf{u} &=& \boldsymbol{\kappa}_u^{-1}\Big( -\boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} + \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v \mathbf{v} -\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\Big) 
\end{eqnarray}

The above equation represents the ideal control action, but the values of $\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)$ and the actual values of the $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u$ and $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v$ matrices are unknown. Therefore, the following super-twisting type control is proposed for the previously presented control objective

%\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:AccionControl}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AccionControl}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}  
    \mathbf{u} &= \hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u^{-1}\Big( -\boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} + \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}\Big) ; \\
    \mathbf{w} &= \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) + \int \mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s}) dt
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

The matrices \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v\) in \eqref{eq:AccionControl} are the online estimates of \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\) and \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\). With \(n=4\), both lie in \(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\). The adaptation gain \(\gamma>0\) is a scalar. Each update has two terms, and both are built from signals available in the loop. The gradient term is the outer product of the regressor with the sliding variable: \(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^{T}\) for the input matrix and \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^{T}\) for the velocity matrix. These products are \(n\times n\), the same size as the estimates. The sign follows \eqref{eq:ModeloLucas}, where \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\) multiplies \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\) multiplies \(-\mathbf{v}\). The second term is a leakage, proportional to \(\|\mathbf{s}\|\) and to the current estimate. It draws the estimate toward zero when \(\mathbf{s}\) is away from the origin, which is the \(\sigma\)-modification used under a nonzero disturbance \cite{lewis1998neural,ge2001stable}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ajuste2}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_u^{T} &= \gamma^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^{T} - \|\mathbf{s}\|\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u^{T},\\
\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_v^{T} &= -\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^{T} - \|\mathbf{s}\|\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:ajuste2} is the law that is integrated. The right-hand side is assembled from \(\mathbf{u}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{s}\), and the current estimate.

\subsection{Inner-Loop Stability}\label{sec:inner_stability}

To test the stability of the inner loop, it is necessary to understand the dynamics represented by $\dot{\mathbf{s}}$.
Now, considering eq.\eqref{eq:Condicion_S} and adding and subtracting $\boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}$, leads to: 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Condicion_S1}
    \dot{\mathbf{s}} =\boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u} +\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\mathbf{u} - \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\dot{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(t) - 
    \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} = 0
\end{equation}

Therefore, replacing \eqref{eq:AccionControl} in the first term of \eqref{eq:Condicion_S1} gives,

% SIGNO: la línea intermedia resta \delta(t). La ec. anterior (eq:Condicion_S1) y el resultado de esta misma ecuación lo suman.
\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:dynamical_equation}
  \nonumber\dot{\mathbf{s}} &=& \boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u^{-1}\Big( -\boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}} + \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) - \int \mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s}) dt\Big) \right) -...\\
   \nonumber &-&\boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u} +\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\mathbf{u}- \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\mathbf{v} \textcolor{red}{- \boldsymbol{\delta}(t)}  - 
    \dot{\mathbf{v}}_d + \boldsymbol{\beta} \tilde{\mathbf{v}}=......\\
    &=&-\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)- \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) - \int \mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s}) dt
\end{eqnarray}



The Parameter error matrices for $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}$, denoted $\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v$ and $\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u$ respectively, are defined as the difference between the optimal output transition matrix $\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v$ and the estimate transition matrix $\boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_v$ of the model  and in the same form for the input matrix ($\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u$ and $\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u$) are defined as:  

\begin{equation}
            \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v = \boldsymbol{\kappa}_v - \boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_v \quad \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u = \boldsymbol{\kappa}_u- \boldsymbol{\hat{\kappa}}_u. \label{eq:error estimation}
\end{equation}

%Taking \eqref{eq:error estimation} into account and rewriting eq. \eqref{eq:dynamical_equation}, we obtain:

To handle the integral action of the super-twisting algorithm without analytical inconsistencies, let us define the auxiliary state vector representing the integral term as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z} = \int_{0}^{t} \mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s}(\tau)) d\tau \implies \dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s})
\end{equation}
Consequently, the control input vector $\mathbf{w}$ can be expressed as $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{\|\mathbf{s}\|}\sign(\mathbf{s}) + \mathbf{z}$. 

Rewriting the control law into the sliding surface dynamics \eqref{eq:dynamical_equation}, the closed-loop error equation becomes:


\begin{equation}\label{eq:SuperficieFinal}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
  \dot{\mathbf{s}} &=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)- \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) -\mathbf{z}\\
  \dot{\mathbf{z}} &=\mathbf{K}_2\sign(\mathbf{s})
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}
%[Inner-loop robust stability]\label{th:inner}
%Consider the velocity-error sliding dynamics \eqref{eq:SuperficieFinal} under the composite adaptive super-twisting control law \eqref{eq:AccionControl} and the adaptation law \eqref{eq:ajuste2}. Suppose that Assumption~\ref{as:physical} holds (so that $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\|\le\bar{\delta}$) and that the super-twisting gain satisfies the matching condition $\mathbf{K}_2\ge\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\|$. Then the sliding variable $\mathbf{s}$ and the parameter error $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u$ are uniformly bounded, $\dot{V}\le 0$, practical stability. %and the velocity-tracking error converges to zero, $\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\to\mathbf{0}$. If, in addition, the regressor $\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)$ is persistently exciting, then $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\to\mathbf{0}$.
[Inner-loop robust stability]\label{th:inner}
Consider the velocity-error sliding dynamics under the adaptive super-twisting control law \eqref{eq:AccionControl} and the adaptation law \eqref{eq:ajuste2}. Suppose that Assumption~\ref{as:parameters} holds, that $\|\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\| \le \delta_o$, and that the time derivative of the perturbation is bounded by $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(t)\|\le\bar{\delta}$. Then, the sliding variable $\mathbf{s}$ and the parameter estimation errors $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v, \tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u$ are uniformly ultimately bounded (UUB), guaranteeing $\dot{V}\le 0$.

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Lyapunov theory is used to perform the stability analysis. First, the following Lyapunov candidate function is proposed,

\begin{equation*}\label{eq:CandidataLyapunov}
    V = \cfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{s} + \cfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v^T)+ \cfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u^T) 
\end{equation*}

Where $tr(.)$ is the trace operator. Now, deriving the above equation, it is obtained,

\begin{equation}
    \dot{V} = \mathbf{s}^T\dot{\mathbf{s}} +\gamma\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_v^T)+ \gamma\,\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u \boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_u^T)
\end{equation}

Replacing \eqref{eq:SuperficieFinal} in the above equation

% SIGNO: eq:SuperficieFinal suma \delta(t). Esta primera línea lo resta. La línea siguiente lo escribe dentro de un paréntesis ya restado, así que el aporte vuelve a ser +s^T \delta(t).
\begin{eqnarray}
   \nonumber \dot{V} &=& \mathbf{s}^T \bigg( -\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}  
     -\Big( \mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) +\mathbf{z} \Big) \textcolor{red}{- \boldsymbol{\delta}(t)} \bigg)+tr(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\gamma\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_v^T)+ tr (\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\gamma \boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_u^T)\nonumber\\
    \dot{V} &=&  -\mathbf{s}^T\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{s}^T\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{s}^T \bigg(\mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) +\mathbf{z}
     -\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)  \bigg)+tr(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\gamma\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_v^T)+ tr (\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\gamma \boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_u^T)
\end{eqnarray}

By the property cyclic of trace, we obtain the following equalities:

\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{s}^T \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v} =tr (\mathbf{s}^T \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v})=tr( \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^T)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{s}^T \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u} =tr (\mathbf{s}^T \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u})=tr( \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^T)
\end{equation}
Based on the properties of transpose and operand, it is obtained,

\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:Lyapunto}
    \nonumber\dot{V} &=& tr( -\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^T)+tr( \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^T) + tr(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\gamma\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_v^T)+ tr (\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\gamma \boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_u^T) -...\\
    \nonumber&-&\mathbf{s}^T\left(\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) +\mathbf{z}
     -\boldsymbol{\delta}(t) \right) \nonumber\\
    \dot{V} &=& tr\Big( \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\big[-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^T+\gamma\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_v^T\big]\Big)+tr\Big( \boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\big[\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^T +\gamma \boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}}_u^T\big]\Big)  -...\\
     \nonumber&-& \mathbf{s}^T\left(\mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{||\mathbf{s}||}\sign(\mathbf{s}) + \mathbf{z}
     -\boldsymbol{\delta}(t) \right)
\end{eqnarray}

The estimates are integrated with \eqref{eq:ajuste2}. The true matrices are constant, so the errors in \eqref{eq:error estimation} satisfy \(\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_u=-\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_u\) and \(\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_v=-\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_v\). Written for those errors, the same law is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ajuste_error}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_u^T &=\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^{T} - \|\mathbf{s}\|\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u^{T},\\
\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}_v^T &=- \gamma^{-1}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^{T} - \|\mathbf{s}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:ajuste_error} is used only in this proof. Substituting it into \eqref{eq:Lyapunto} cancels the regressor terms \(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^{T}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^{T}\) and leaves the leakage,
\begin{eqnarray}\label{eq:Lyap_trace}
   \dot{V} &=& \gamma\|\mathbf{s}\|\,\mathrm{tr}\big(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_v^{T}\big)+\gamma\|\mathbf{s}\|\,\mathrm{tr}\big(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_u^{T}\big)-\mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{K}_1\sqrt{\|\mathbf{s}\|}\sign(\mathbf{s})- \mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{z}
     +\mathbf{s}^T\boldsymbol{\delta}(t).
\end{eqnarray}
From \eqref{eq:error estimation}, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_i=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_i-\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_i\). With the bounds of Assumption~\ref{as:parameters} and the Frobenius norm,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{tr}\big(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_i\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_i^{T}\big)
\le -\left(\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_i\|-\frac{\kappa_{iM}}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{\kappa_{iM}^{2}}{4},
\qquad i\in\{u,v\}.
\end{equation}
The squared parameter errors therefore enter \(\dot{V}\) with a negative sign. The terms that remain positive are proportional to \(\|\mathbf{s}\|\) and to the disturbance. For \(\mathbf{K}_1\) and \(\mathbf{K}_2\) chosen against the bounds of Assumption~\ref{as:physical}, those terms are dominated outside a compact set, and \(\dot{V}\le 0\) there. The sliding variable \(\mathbf{s}\), the integral state \(\mathbf{z}\), and the parameter errors \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u,\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\) are uniformly ultimately bounded.

\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Persistent excitation]\label{rem:pe}
Theorem~\ref{th:inner} bounds the parameter errors. Convergence of those errors to zero is a property of a different update. Consider a pure gradient law, with the leakage removed from \eqref{eq:ajuste2} and with \(\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)=\mathbf{0}\). The update is then driven only by \(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{s}^{T}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{s}^{T}\). Once \(\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}\), both products vanish, and the estimates stop. Any error that satisfies \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\mathbf{u}(t)=\mathbf{0}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{0}\) along the trajectory produces the same \(\dot{\mathbf{s}}\), so the component of \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\) and \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\) orthogonal to the recorded regressor is left unchanged.

Persistent excitation is the condition that removes this ambiguity. For a regressor \(\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\) it requires constants \(T,\alpha>0\) such that
\begin{equation}
\int_{t}^{t+T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\tau)\boldsymbol{\phi}(\tau)^{\top}\,d\tau \ge \alpha I
\label{eq:pe}
\end{equation}
for every \(t\ge 0\). The vector \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) has to span the entries of \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_u\) and \(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_v\). For the full matrices this is a Kronecker regressor built from \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\). Under the ideal law, \eqref{eq:pe} is what forces \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_u\) and \(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\kappa}}_v\) to zero, because the only parameter error compatible with a zero residual is the zero error.

Equation \eqref{eq:ajuste2} retains the leakage \(-\|\mathbf{s}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{T}\), and \(\dot{\mathbf{s}}\) retains \(\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\). The leakage balances the gradient at a nonzero parameter error, and the disturbance shifts \(\dot{\mathbf{s}}\) in the same way as a parameter mismatch. Both effects remain when \eqref{eq:pe} holds. Every term of \eqref{eq:ajuste2} is also scaled by a signal that vanishes with \(\mathbf{s}\), so the estimates freeze once the sliding variable has entered a neighborhood of the origin. The result proved for this law is the uniform ultimate boundedness of Theorem~\ref{th:inner}.
\end{remark}

The control objective is for the load to follow a reference trajectory in space. For controller validation, no information from the dynamic model was considered, nor was any identification performed. Two simulation scenarios were defined. In the first group, an ascending circular reference trajectory was used to compare the system's behavior with only the kinematic controller and with the dynamic compensator. In the second group, a more demanding reference was considered, and prescribed wind disturbances were applied over two time intervals. In this test, the aerodynamic disturbances acted on the UAV, the cable, and the payload. To evaluate the performance of the proposed controller, three complementary metrics are used to quantify the oscillatory behavior of the cable–payload system. From \eqref{eq:cable_vector}, the first metric is the cable inclination angle, defined as
% REV1-C7: Esta definición de $\mathbf{h}$ contradice \eqref{eq:cable_vector}. Unificar como $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_l$ o renombrar el vector de métricas para evitar errores de signo.
\begin{equation}
\alpha(t)=\mathrm{atan2}\!\left(\sqrt{h_x^2+h_y^2},-h_z\right),
\end{equation}
which quantifies the extent to which the cable deviates from its vertical orientation and signifies the amplitude of the swinging payload. This criterion has been extensively used in various aerial cargo transport problems because it directly relates to the amplitude of the oscillation concerning the vertical position. The second metric is the horizontal displacement of the payload relative to the UAV, defined as
\begin{equation}
\rho(t)=\sqrt{h_x^2+h_y^2},
\end{equation}
which corresponds to the lateral distance of the payload from the vertical axis passing through the UAV. Finally, the dynamic behavior of the swing is characterized through the tangential relative velocity between the UAV and the payload. Let $\mathbf{v}_{rel}=\mathbf{v}_L-\mathbf{v}_U$ be the relative velocity and $\hat{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{h}/\|\mathbf{h}\|$ the unit cable direction. The tangential component is computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_{\perp}=\mathbf{v}_{rel}-(\mathbf{v}_{rel}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{h}})\hat{\mathbf{h}}.
\end{equation}
While $\alpha(t)$ and $\rho(t)$ describe the geometric magnitude of the oscillation, $\|\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\|$ captures the dynamic energy associated with the pendular motion. The joint analysis of these metrics provides a comprehensive assessment of both the amplitude and damping characteristics of the payload oscillations. Tracking performance is evaluated using RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) metrics, whereas oscillatory behavior is quantified using RMS (Root Mean Square) values of the swing-related variables.

\subsection{Payload trajectory tracking}

A smooth, continuously differentiable reference trajectory was defined for the load to evaluate the system under representative air transport conditions without introducing discontinuities to the control signals.
\begin{eqnarray}
    \mathbf{x}_d = \begin{bmatrix} x_d \\ y_d \\ z_d \\ \psi_d\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \sin(2\pi \frac{t}{10}) \ [m] \\  3 \cos(2\pi \frac{t}{10}) \ [m]\\ 6+\frac{t}{10} \ [m] \\ \arctan(\dot{y}_d / \dot{x}_d) \ [rad]\end{bmatrix}
\end{eqnarray}
It should be noted that in any trajectory-tracking problem in which there is a requirement for lateral acceleration, the rope cannot be kept vertical. Rather, there has to be some form of inclination. In the case of circular and/or elliptical paths, this is in the form of a nonzero quasi-static inclination that provides the necessary acceleration for the circular motion. Therefore, the raw quantities $\alpha(t)$ and $\rho(t)$ contain both a steady-state component imposed by the trajectory and a truly oscillatory component associated with payload swing. As a result, the direct use of $RMS_\alpha$ and $RMS_\rho$ may overestimate the actual oscillation level. This quasi-static component can be recognized as a common-mode effect in oscillation-related quantities, as it depends mainly on the reference trajectory rather than the control strategy.

\subsection{Benchmark Against a Published Baseline Controller}
\label{sec:benchmark_smc}

This subsection compares the adaptive super-twisting velocity loop with a first-order sliding-mode controller (SMC) on the ascending circular trajectory. The baseline is the law proposed in \cite{kusznir2020sliding} for cable-suspended load transport with a quadrotor. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_smc} is the index of this comparison. Figures~\ref{fig:benchmark_metrics} and~\ref{fig:benchmark_control} are the corresponding time histories.

First-order sliding-mode control is a natural and widely documented reference for suspended load transport, as it shares the same family of sliding modes as the adaptive super-twisting law of the dynamic compensator and directly answers the question of what is gained by moving from discontinuous first-order action to continuous second-order action. Unlike fully coupled geometric SE(3) designs or model-based passivity approaches, a first-order SMC does not require the full coupled UAV-cable-load dynamic model, can be implemented in the same standardized command interface used in this article (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ModeloLucas}), and is representative of a significant and well-cited family of controllers for overhead load transport. Therefore, the reference is reproducible, computationally comparable, and technically fair with respect to the proposed scheme.

To ensure a fair comparison that isolates the effect of the dynamic compensator, both schemes share (i) the same high-fidelity simulator described at the beginning of Section ~\ref{sec:simulations}; (ii) the same upward circular payload reference trajectory; (iii) the same physical parameters listed in Table ~\ref{tab:sim_params}; (iv) the same initial condition; and (v) the same kinematic controller with quasi-static oscillation compensation (Section ~\ref{sec:kinematic}). The only element that differs between the two implementations is the velocity-tracking law of the dynamic compensator. The proposed controller uses the adaptive super-twisting law defined in \eqref{eq:AccionControl}--\eqref{eq:ajuste2}, while the baseline uses a first-order sliding-mode action of the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{SMC}}
=
-\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{SMC}}\,\dfrac{\mathbf{s}}{\|\mathbf{s}\|+\varphi},
\label{eq:smc_baseline}
\end{equation}
built on the same sliding surface $\mathbf{s}$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}, where $\mathbf{K}_{\mathrm{SMC}}\!>\!\mathbf{0}$ is a diagonal gain matrix, and $\varphi>0$ is a boundary-layer thickness used to attenuate chattering, following the classical practice of \cite{kusznir2020sliding}. This substitution isolates the contribution of the adaptive super-twisting law and its adaptation law from the effect of the kinematic controller.

Both controllers share the kinematic controller, so each entry of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_smc} is the effect of the inner-loop law. The reading of that table is given with Figures~\ref{fig:benchmark_metrics} and~\ref{fig:benchmark_control}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusiones}

This study developed the robust adaptive cascaded controller of Section~\ref{sec:kinematic} for transporting a cable-suspended load with a single UAV. Its main contribution lies in the coordinated operation of these two loops. Instead of treating every cable tilt as an unwanted oscillation, the outer loop considers the quasi-static tilt required by the commanded acceleration and compensates only for motion relative to that equilibrium. The inner loop then addresses parametric mismatch, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances. This modular formulation reduces the reliance on an exact model of the coupled UAV-cable-payload dynamics. It avoids constrained online optimization, making it a relatively lightweight alternative to payload-oriented trajectory tracking.

Stability analysis was performed separately for each loop. Under the idealized condition of perfect velocity tracking ($\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{u}_k$), Theorem~\ref{th:outer} establishes the input-to-state stability of the outer loop error cascade, with local exponential stability recovered in the unperturbed case. Independently, Theorem~\ref{th:inner} shows that the sliding variable, the super-twisting integral state, and the parameter errors are uniformly ultimately bounded. Remark~\ref{rem:pe} explains why persistent excitation of the regressor is not invoked to conclude that those parameter errors converge to zero. These two results are complementary, but not yet unified: the formal demonstration that interconnecting the two loops (where the inner loop achieves approximate, not exact, velocity tracking) preserves the stability margins established for the idealized outer loop (e.g., using a singular perturbation or ISS cascade argument on the actual closed-loop system) is left for future work. Therefore, the simulation results presented here should be interpreted as strong empirical support for this composition, complementing, rather than replacing, a fully formalized interconnection demonstration.

The numerical comparison is the one already given in Section~\ref{sec:simulations}. On the ascending circle, Table~\ref{tab:metrics} is the difference between the kinematic controller and the cascade, and the associated records are Figures~\ref{fig:CinematicoII_Variabes}--\ref{fig:Compensador_ErroresVelocidad}. On the lemniscate, Figures~\ref{fig:Viento_ErroresControl}--\ref{fig:Viento_ErroresVelocidad} are the position, swing, and velocity-error records through the two prescribed wind intervals; that run has no scalar index. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_smc} and Figures~\ref{fig:benchmark_metrics} and~\ref{fig:benchmark_control} are the comparison of the two inner-loop laws.

The immediate next step is therefore high-fidelity numerical validation. This effort will include implementation on an onboard PX4/ROS\,2 platform, reconstruction of $\mathbf{h}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{h}}$ from lightweight sensing, and explicit characterization of measurement noise, delay, computation time, and actuator saturation. Indoor motion-capture experiments with different payload masses will assess adaptation under genuine parametric variation, followed by outdoor tests under natural wind. The same tracking and swing metrics used here will enable direct comparison between simulation and flight data. Future studies should also examine more aggressive trajectories, elastic or variable-length cables, different payload geometries, and a broader benchmark set that includes geometric, passivity-based, and predictive controllers under matched experimental conditions.
